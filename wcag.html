Duration: 3 weeks

The standard that defines accessibility on the web is itself one of the least accessible documents you'll ever read.

WCAG, the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, is the legal benchmark for whether a digital service works for everyone, including people with disabilities. In Norway, failing to meet it means enforcement and heavy fines. But the guidelines are written in dense, compliance-first language that most designers struggle to navigate. We were a class of 20 students who decided to do something about it. Split into five groups, each took a section of WCAG and redesigned how it's explained. Our group took Understandable, the principle covering clear language, predictable behaviour, and helpful error handling. Which gave us a fittingly strange task: explain, in plain terms, the part of an accessibility standard that's supposed to be about being plain in the first place.

00 Challenge & my role

Challenge

The challenge was simple: make WCAG more accessible for the people who actually need to use it. Today, WCAG is a legal standard: failure to meet it can result in daily fines. SAS faced exactly that in 2018.

The irony is that the WCAG 2.2 website itself is a terrible way to access the information intuitively, and they're the ones that determine whether Uutilsynet comes after you.

Our tutor saw the opportunity. With nearly 20 designers/students in the room at the same time (something that rarely happens), we used it to give something back. Each group took a section of WCAG and tried to explain it as clearly and visually as possible.

My role

I owned section 3.2, Predictable, responsible for explaining it to a complete beginner. I took the initiative to create illustrations for each section across the resource: introduction, readable, predictable, and input assistance. I also produced visual examples covering criteria 3.2.1 through 3.2.6, later handing 3.2.1 through 3.2.3 to a teammate who joined the project later.

Beyond my own section, I was actively involved in shaping the overall structure and format, contributing to how we built a consistent layout that worked across the whole group's output.

01 Analysis

At first glance the WCAG website seems reasonable enough. But for someone who actually has to apply the guidelines, the cognitive load is significant. There are no visual examples. The text is dense and repetitive. It speaks in generalities, and when you dig into something like input assistance, you get more heavy explanation without any grounding in how it actually looks or works in practice.

02 Design

We know people understand rules better when they can see them. So we structured our section around two things: clear visual examples, and short contextual explanations built on a simple pattern — what should be true, and why it matters. From there we designed introductions, illustrations, and cards that made each guideline tangible rather than theoretical.
